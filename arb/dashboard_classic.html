Mathematics

Every rule the engine enforces is a consequence of one identity: an option's price is the discounted integral of its payoff against the risk-neutral density q. Differentiating that integral in the strike turns butterflies into densities, vertical spreads into probabilities, boxes into discount factors and put–call parity into the forward. The tables below evaluate each of these on live mid prices, once every two seconds.

1. Pricing as an integral

(1) C(K)= D(T) ∫K∞ (S−K) q(S)dS , P(K)= D(T) ∫0K (K−S) q(S)dS
(2) D(T)= exp(− ∫0T r(t)dt )=e−rT , ∫0∞ q(S)dS =1

Here D(T) is the discount factor to expiry, r the financing rate and q the density the market is implicitly quoting. The engine never assumes r: it reads it off the chain, as in (3), one rate per expiry, and discounts money it will receive at the borrow rate and money it must pay at the lend rate.

2. The box integrates the whole density: discounting and the implied rate

(3) [C(K1)− C(K2)]+ [P(K2)− P(K1)] =D(T) ∫0∞ (K2−K1) q(S)dS =D(T) (K2−K1) ⇒ r^= −1T lnBK2−K1

A box's payoff is the constant K2 − K1 whatever happens, so its price B is that constant discounted — the density integrates to one and drops out. The ratio B/D(K2 − K1) must be exactly 1; the median over the chain gives the market's own rate, and each chain is discounted at its own.

3. Butterflies are the density (Breeden–Litzenberger, 1978)

(4) ∂2C ∂K2 =D(T) q(K)≥0 , q^(Ki)= C(Ki−1) −2C(Ki) +C(Ki+1) D(T)h2

The second difference of call prices across three equally spaced strikes is a butterfly. Its price is D·h2 times the density at the middle strike, so it can never be negative: a butterfly quoted below zero is the market offering to pay you to hold a non-negative payoff. Bars below the axis are exactly the convexity violations the BUTTERFLY scanner trades.

4. Vertical spreads are probabilities

(5) −∂C∂K =D(T) ∫K∞ q(S)dS =D(T) [1−Q(K)] ⇒ 0≤ C(K1)− C(K2) D(T) (K2−K1) ≤1

The first difference is the tail probability, so a call spread is worth between nothing and its discounted width; the same holds for puts with Q in place of 1 − Q. The step curve is the distribution function Q̂ the chain implies, read off adjacent call mids; any step that runs backwards is a VERTICAL violation.

Implied distribution function Q̂(K) = 1 + [C(Ki+1) − C(Ki)] / D h, from live call mids. Dashed: the spot.

5. Put–call parity is the first moment

(6) C(K)−P(K) =D(T) ∫0∞ (S−K) q(S)dS =D(T) (F−K) ⇒ F^=K+ C(K)−P(K) D(T)

Subtracting the two integrals in (1) leaves the mean of the density, the forward F. Every strike therefore implies the same futures price; where a listed future settles on the same day, the gap between F̂ and its bid or ask is the PCP conversion/reversal edge, before costs.

6. Edge, cost, and the band

(7) gross= ∑i=1n σipiqi −D(T) minS Π(S) , net=gross− ∑i=1n ci
(8) ci=b +τSTT 1sellpiqi +τexchpiqi +τGST (b+τexchpiqi) +τstamp 1buypiqi +τSEBIpiqi +sqi
(9) ρ= netS·q · 365max(T,1day) , trade⇔ 2%≤ρ≤5% per year

In (7), σi = +1 for a leg sold and −1 for a leg bought, pi is the price crossed (bid to sell, ask to buy), qi the quantity and Π the settlement payoff, which is piecewise linear with kinks only at strikes, so its minimum is found exactly by evaluating it at every strike. (8) is the statutory cost of one leg: brokerage b, STT on sales, exchange fee, GST on the two, stamp duty on purchases, the SEBI fee and slippage s per unit. (9) is the band: the net edge as an annualised excess yield on the contract value the structure controls, which for a box is exactly how far the implied rate sits from fair. The table shows what 2% and 5% per year come to on each chain today, in rupees and in index points.

7. Margin and the stake

(10) M=max( Msettle, MAngel) , Msettle=max( −∑i=1n σipiqi , −minSΠ(S) ,0) , lots=min( ⌊stakeM⌋, depth,12)

MAngel is what Angel One's margin calculator returns for the legs as a basket (SPAN plus exposure, net of the hedge benefit); it is what the account must actually hold, and is the "invested" figure on every trade. The stake is fixed at and is never exceeded: a structure whose single lot needs more is not traded.

8. The futures curve and calendar carry

(11) F(T)=S erT , r1,2= ln(F2/F1) T2−T1 , fair spread=F1 (er(T2−T1)−1)

Each future implies a carry rate to its own date, and two futures imply a forward carry between their dates. A calendar spread is rich when that forward carry exceeds the configured financing rate and cheap when it falls short; the box-implied rate of each expiry is shown beside it for comparison. Shown as analytics only: unlike a box or a parity trade, a calendar spread's payoff is not locked in at entry, so the engine does not book it as arbitrage.
